# Derivational suffix and -ik verb morphology

## Problem

Morphologically disambiguated corpus data has a systematic problem for this pattern: low-frequency 1sg indefinite forms in `-m` get mis-tagged as definite. `-m` is homophonous between the two readings (it's the regular 1sg definite ending for every verb, and the special `-ik`-conjugation indefinite ending for this class), and the automatic tagger resolves the ambiguity wrong often enough, especially for rare forms, that it's visible even for verbs that can never grammatically take a definite reading at all.

Concretely: Webcorpus2 has exactly two matches for `nyílom` (the 1sg indefinite of `nyílik`, "opens", a verb with no possible transitive/definite use), and both are tagged `[/V][Prs.Def.1Sg]`. The real, levelled indefinite use is there in the corpus; the tagger just calls it definite.

What do? Use context to disambiguate instead of trusting the tag. Reading a KWIC line and judging definite vs indefinite from the actual sentence is a good task to hand to AI help: cheap to scale, and checkable against real examples rather than a black-box tag.

## Workflow

1. **`script/subset_verbs.R`** — reads Webcorpus2, keeps every verb (1sg and 3sg indefinite forms) and classifies it into a derivational-suffix class (`lik`, `szik`, `zik`, `odik` = `-odik/-edik/-ödik` and `-kodik/-kedik/-ködik`, `stem` = everything else — the `szik`/`lik` rules require more than one/two syllables specifically to exclude old monosyllabic-root verbs like `eszik`/`iszik`/`alszik` that only coincidentally end in the right letters). Screens out verb/noun homograph risk on the `-k` form (e.g. `horgászok`, both "I fish" and "anglers", via a plural-noun lookup); the equivalent check on the derived `-m` form (via a 1sg-possessive-noun lookup) is computed but found negligible across the corpus as a whole — it doesn't catch specific bad cases like `telom`/`illem` (see step 4), which only a concordance read exposes.
   An earlier version of this script collapsed each lemma+tag combination down to its single most-frequent attested form before sampling (`group_by(lemma,xpostag) |> slice(1)`), intended to pick "the most frequent attested form" per lemma. This silently dropped any lemma whose `-m` form happened to be tagged more frequently than its `-k` form under the same `NDef.1Sg` tag (e.g. `cselekszem`, 3938 occurrences, vs `cselekszek`, 301) — precisely the heavily `-m`-biased verbs this whole project is trying to sample. Fixed by keeping every individual form row rather than collapsing by lemma; the `-k` form just needs its own frequency above 10 to qualify, regardless of how its `-m` counterpart compares. Side effect: a handful of lemmas have two attested `-k` stem variants (the `cselekszik`/`cselekedik`-type syncopation alternation, e.g. `igyekszek`/`igyekezek`) and can now be sampled twice; `script/tally_verbs.R` deduplicates by lemma before the final join so this doesn't double-count them.
   Draws a stratified sample (40 lemmas per suffix class) from the qualifying `-k` forms and builds a `[km]`-alternation regex from them. Writes `dat/ik_sample.tsv`, `dat/ik_query.txt`, `dat/verbs.tsv` (the full candidate pool), `dat/counts.tsv`.
2. **MNSZ2-UD concordance search** (external, not scripted here) — the regex is run as a literal string search against MNSZ2-UD, a different, independently tagged corpus, returning real sentence context for every hit. The export format is a pipe-delimited `.txt` (`doc#N | sentence text`, the hit marked inline as `<coll>...</coll>`), not the CSV format used in earlier rounds.
3. **`script/subset_results.R`** — parses the pipe-delimited export into `Reference`/`Left`/`KWIC`/`Right` columns, derives a `lemma` column from the KWIC form, and keeps every lemma that got at least one hit — no per-class balancing (the GLMM's per-lemma random intercept handles the resulting unequal group sizes). Writes `dat/concordance_mnsz2_tidy.tsv`.
4. **`dat/lemma_transitivity_decisions.tsv`** — hand review of every sampled lemma that got a corpus hit: does the verb ever grammatically license a direct accusative object at all, under any construction? Most govern datives, obliques, or infinitival complements and never do. A handful are additionally marked `EXCLUDE_data_quality`: not a transitivity question but a homograph problem severe enough that the lemma's corpus hits are mostly noise, not the verb at all — `ellik`'s hits are almost all a mangled "ellen"/proper-noun fragment, `illik`'s collide with the noun "illem" (etiquette) *and* the surname "Illek" on both the `-k` and `-m` side, `telik`'s `-m`/`-k` forms collide with "telo", slang for "phone". These lemmas are dropped entirely, not just marked indefinite. Each time the sample is rebuilt, lemmas that overlap with a previous round's reviewed set carry their decision forward automatically rather than being re-judged from scratch; only genuinely new lemmas need fresh review.
5. **`script/classify_definiteness.R`** — classifies every surviving `-m` hit as definite or indefinite from its concordance context. This is the part that changed the most across rounds, and the script's own comments document each fix in detail; the headline points:
   - Runs on **every** `-m` hit, not just lemmas pre-flagged as object-capable. Earlier rounds skipped the context check for "never takes an object" lemmas, which is wrong: a preverb can make a strictly intransitive verb definite-transitive (`alszik` never takes an object bare, but `átalszom a délutánt`, "I sleep through the afternoon", is grammatical and definite — the indefinite counterpart is ungrammatical). If the preverb separates from the verb in the sentence (routine under negation, focus, or object-fronting), a literal search on the bare verb catches these hits, and skipping the context check would have silently miscounted them.
   - Definite triggers: accusative pronouns/demonstratives (`azt`, `ezt`, `őt`, `ugyanazt`, `mindezt`, `mindazt`, `magamat`), a definite article followed by an accusative-marked noun (tolerating an intervening adjective or particle), a possessive-accusative shape (`j` + vowel + `t`, e.g. `ÁFÁját` — this one needs no article or capitalization at all), and a narrow hardcoded patch for `alszik`'s `álmát`/`álmomat` idiom and for the one confirmed proper-noun case (`Nitzsét`/`Nietzschét`).
   - Forced-indefinite triggers: interrogative, relative, and indefinite accusative pronouns (`mit`, `kit`, `amit`, `akit`, `valamit`, `valakit`, `semmit`, `senkit`, `mindenkit`, `egyet`) can never co-occur with definite conjugation, so they override any other signal. A small set of lemmas (`tartozik`, `nézelődik`, `szerencsétlenkedik`, `érdeklődik`, `kínlódik`, `alakoskodik`, `lábatlankodik`, `közlekedik`, `hülyéskedik`) are hardcoded to always-indefinite, confirmed never to take an accusative object under any construction, because they produced specific, checkable false positives otherwise (mostly the article+noun pattern misfiring on a bare dative/allative noun that happens to end in `-at`/`-et` as part of its own stem, like `kötet`, `csapat`).
   - Known false-positive guards: `-ért` ("for the sake of") and `a fenét` ("the heck!", a fixed exclamatory idiom) both grammatically resemble article+accusative-noun but aren't objects. A stoplist excludes sentence-initial discourse connectives (`sőt`, `tehát`, `hát`) and bare quantity/degree accusative adverbials (`sokat`, `kicsit`, `nagyot`, `mélyeket`, `keveset`, `ennyit`, `rengeteget`...) from the capitalized-proper-noun check, since Hungarian's productive degree-adverbial construction looks identical in shape to an object but doesn't correlate with definiteness at all — this stoplist is necessarily incomplete (open-class formation).
   - A general "capitalized word = proper noun = definite" rule was tried and dropped: it can't distinguish a real proper noun (`Nietzschét`, confirmed definite) from a common noun that's capitalized only for sitting at a sentence start (`Bokrot`, "a bush", confirmed indefinite) — both look identical in surface form. Kept only where it's structurally safe (right side of the verb, where the verb itself guarantees non-sentence-initial position) or explicitly confirmed by hand.
   - The MNSZ export's context window is genuinely short (median ~10 characters each side, max 24) — some indefinite calls are hitting a real information ceiling, not a gap the heuristic could close.
   - Writes `dat/objcap_rows_classified.tsv` (one row per classified `-m` hit).
6. **`script/build_judged_concordance.R`** — merges the classification back onto the *entire* original concordance (both `-k` and `-m`, every lemma including excluded ones, labelled with the exclusion reason) for manual spot-checking. Writes `dat/concordance_mnsz2_judged.tsv`. Row-matched via a stable `row_id`, not by content columns — a small number of `(Reference,Left,KWIC,Right,lemma)` tuples are genuine duplicate corpus hits, and a content-based join either drops too many or too few of them.
7. **`script/tally_verbs.R`** — drops the excluded lemmas and the rows classified definite (matched by `row_id`, same reasoning as step 6), deduplicates the sample metadata by lemma (see step 1 — a lemma can have two attested `-k` stem variants and otherwise gets joined in twice), tallies `-k` vs `-m` per lemma, joins frequency and suffix-class metadata, computes `p_k` and the `-k`/`-m` log-odds. Writes `dat/sample_counts.tsv` (177 lemmas this round, unevenly split across suffix classes: `lik` 35, `odik` 40, `stem` 38, `szik` 25, `zik` 39).
8. **`script/sample_down_concordance.R`** — balances the tally down to the same number of lemmas per suffix class (25, matching the smallest, `-szik`), so the model isn't comparing a 25-verb class against a 40-verb one. Also recomputes `log_1sg` (log frequency of the specific 1sg form) directly from `verbs.tsv` with `summarise(freq = sum(freq), .by = ...)`, which sums frequency across a lemma's two stem variants (see step 1) rather than arbitrarily keeping one — a cleaner fix for the same duplication issue than step 7's `distinct()`, since it doesn't discard either variant's token count. Writes `dat/final_counts.tsv` (125 lemmas, 25 per class).

## Results

`script/analysis_verbs.R` fits a Bayesian mixed-effects logistic regression (`rstanarm::stan_glmer`, 4 chains × 4000 iterations) on `dat/final_counts.tsv`: `cbind(k,m) ~ suffix + log_1sg + llfpm10 + (1|lemma)`. The outcome is each verb's `-k` vs `-m` count; suffix class is the predictor of interest, `log_1sg` and `llfpm10` (log lemma frequency, per million) are included as controls, and a per-lemma random intercept absorbs individual-verb variation so the suffix comparison isn't driven by a handful of extreme items. `emmeans::emmeans()` + `tidybayes::gather_emmeans_draws()` give the full posterior distribution of the marginal log-odds of `-k` for each suffix class, plotted as a half-eye (`viz/bayes_halfeye_suffix.png`) rather than collapsed to a point estimate.

On the balanced 125-lemma sample (25 per suffix class), converged cleanly (max R̂ 1.01, min ESS 722/1600, 0 parameters over threshold, 0 divergent transitions). `-lik` sits clearly apart from every other class, and with equal group sizes the remaining four are even more tightly clustered than in the unbalanced 177-lemma version:

| suffix | median p(-k) | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|
| `-lik` | 0.89 | [0.76, 0.96] |
| `-zik` | 0.36 | [0.20, 0.56] |
| `csak tő` | 0.36 | [0.20, 0.56] |
| `-odik/edik/ödik` | 0.34 | [0.18, 0.55] |
| `-szik` | 0.32 | [0.16, 0.54] |

`-lik` verbs level to `-k` far more than any other class; the other four now overlap almost completely (32–36%), the cleanest version of this result so far. A previous round (before the `subset_verbs.R` fix described in step 1) found `-szik` anomalously elevated (≈0.72, close to `-lik`) — that round's `-szik` sample was missing exactly the heavily `-m`-biased verbs the bug was dropping (`cselekszik`, `igyekszik`, `emlékszik`, `haragszik`...). With those verbs restored and the sample balanced, `-szik` sits with the other three at the low end instead. The earlier elevated estimate should be treated as a sampling artifact, not a real effect.

Worth flagging: the covariate estimates moved too. `llfpm10` (lemma frequency) now has a clearly credible *negative* effect (−0.98, 95% CI [−1.45, −0.52], from `dat/best_model_summary.tsv`) where earlier rounds found no credible effect either direction — more frequent lemmas now favour `-m` more, holding suffix class constant. `log_1sg` is small and sits close to zero (−0.28, [−0.56, 0.01]). These shifted because the sample composition changed substantially (the `subset_verbs.R` fix, then balancing); worth a sanity check before leaning on them in the text, since neither is the paper's main claim.
